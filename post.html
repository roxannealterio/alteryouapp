Progressive overload: the only rule that matters

Roxy Alterio 5 min read

If you take one thing from anything we ever write, take this. The reason a program works isn’t that it left you wrecked, or that you sweated, or that you could barely walk down the stairs the next day. It works because, week over week, you asked your body to do a little more than it did last time. That’s progressive overload, and it’s the entire game.

Soreness is feedback. It’s not a scorecard.

Doing more can look like a lot of things. An extra rep at the same weight. The same reps with two more kilos. One more set. A slower, more controlled tempo on the way down. A fuller range of motion than you managed a month ago. All of them are progress, and none of them require you to be destroyed by the end of the session.

Why soreness fools everyone

Muscle soreness mostly tells you that you did something your body wasn’t used to, not that you did something effective. A brand new movement will make you sore whether or not it builds anything. And the movements you have done for months, the ones actually driving your results, often stop making you sore at all. If you chase the ache, you’ll keep swapping exercises to feel it, and quietly never get stronger at any of them.

What to do instead

Pick a program and stay on it long enough to add load. Write down what you lifted. Next time, try to beat it by something small and specific. When a weight starts to feel easy for all your reps, that’s the signal to go up, not to add another finisher. This is exactly why the app nudges you to go heavier when your logged sets say you’re ready, and why every program tells you how many weeks it needs before you begin.

Strength is built in boring, repeatable increments. That’s not the disappointing part. That’s the reassuring part, because it means it doesn’t depend on motivation, or a perfect week, or how sore you got. It depends on showing up and adding a little. You can do that.
